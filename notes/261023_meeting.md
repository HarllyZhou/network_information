# Meeting notes

**23 October 2026** · Hongyi and Zhenghua

Contagious beliefs in a production network, and how to put diagnostic expectations to the data.

---

## In brief

The meeting was about a research design in which beliefs move between optimism and pessimism, then travel through input–output links. Hongyi set out an SIS-style epidemic frame for that movement. Both sides treated diagnostic expectations as the distortion to embed, because the distortion is a single parameter and is easy to carry through the model. Zhenghua pressed on identification: what overreaction is in the data, and how it changes over time. They closed by splitting the next two weeks. Zhenghua designs the empirical test. Hongyi works through the inflation paper and how an inflation target and monetary policy sit inside the framework.

---

## What was discussed

### The wiki

Hongyi walked through the research wiki: how a paper is filed, how the index groups it, and how topics such as production networks, belief-driven cycles, and information frictions accumulate sources. The demonstration included Federal Reserve material and the related analysis pages. They also looked at a neoclassical model in which beliefs move through two channels.

### Contagion and associativeness

The two channels, taken from Flynn and Sastry, were kept distinct.

- **Contagion.** A narrative spreads because it is already common. The chance of holding it depends on the share of agents who hold it.
- **Associativeness.** A narrative spreads because it lines up with outcomes. If what an agent expects is too far from what arrives, the agent can switch.

The spread need not be linear in the share of optimists. The share itself can change how fast the narrative moves.

### The SIS law of motion

Hongyi described an SIS economy with two groups, the susceptible and the infected, and a difference equation for the size of each group. One parameter governs infection and another governs recovery. The chance that an uninfected agent meets an infected agent is built from expectations. The diagnosticity parameter \(\theta\) scales how strongly that process pushes prices. Once a diagnostic firm posts a distorted price, the input–output structure carries the distortion to its customers. Zhenghua asked how this differs from the noise in Professor Huo’s paper.

### Why diagnostic expectations

Two questions stayed open.

1. Why diagnostic expectations, rather than another departure from full-information rational expectations.
2. How macro and micro shocks aggregate, and where overreaction and underreaction show up at each level.

The working answer on the first question was practical. Diagnosticity is one parameter, and the overreaction to news has a closed form, so it can be placed inside the epidemic and the network without a new information technology. The second question was left as something the model has to deliver: the same \(\theta\) should be able to produce different degrees of overreaction once shocks are aggregated through the network.

### From an epidemic to a price

The discussion separated a quantitative difference (how large the infected share is) from a qualitative one (whether the long run is a stable interior share or a narrative that takes over). Recovery can be dynamic, so the aggregate infection rate is not a constant. The price path runs through upstream prices. Sectoral goods are combined by competitive aggregators, and the resulting price is the object that downstream firms see. Expectation formation then has the same two channels as in the narrative paper: associativeness with respect to outcomes, and contagion with respect to the current share of beliefs.

### How to test it

A diagnostic firm overreacts, so its forecast errors should be predictable. The proposed check is a regression of that predictability in firm-level survey data, with a sector-level diagnosticity series built so the same object can move over time. The research would start from the empirical evidence, then a proof of concept for the sector-level series, rather than from a fully calibrated model.
